⏺ Short answer: the change is superficial for your argument, would be expensive 
  to implement, and would actually weaken the MDL evidence. Don't restructure 
  the corpus. There's a nearly-free probe experiment you can add instead if you 
  want the ecological connection to be explicit.

  Here's the reasoning:

  The Sally-Anne test is a measurement instrument, not the learning problem.
  Your thesis models the acquisition of belief as a concept: how a learner with
  only domain-general primitives comes to possess a belief-shaped abstraction.
  The developmental test is the diagnostic administered afterward to check
  whether a child possesses it. Making your training tasks look like the
  diagnostic would conflate acquisition with assessment — children don't learn
  ToM by answering Sally-Anne questions. What your experiment shares with the
  developmental story is the input side: the child, like your learner, observes
  complete scenes (Anne moving the marble, Sally returning) and has to build a
  compressed internal model of them. Full-trajectory reconstruction is exactly
  "build an internal model of the observed scene."

  Your current format subsumes the test format. In your goal-displacement family
  (which your own code comments already label Sally-Anne — tasks_minds.py:356),
  the agent acts on its stale belief: it seeks the goal's old location. So the
  final frame of every solved trajectory literally is the Sally-Anne answer —
  where the agent ends up looking. A learner that reproduces the whole
  trajectory has strictly more competence than one that emits the last frame: it
  can answer the test question by running its model forward and reading off the
  final state. This is the Marr-style defense to write in the thesis — you're
  modeling the computational-level competence (a generative model of scenes
  containing believers), and the behavioral test answer is a one-line query
  against that competence.

  Switching to final-frame supervision would weaken the MDL argument, not 
  tighten it. Your entire case rests on belief being the shortest program
  consistent with the data, with rivals extensionally excluded (all your
  _*_explainable rejection machinery). Full-trajectory supervision is what pins
  down the intermediate dynamics and kills shallow rivals. If the target were
  only the last frame, the space of programs matching each task explodes —
  heuristics that skip belief modeling entirely ("agent ends up adjacent to the
  goal's old cell") would match many tasks, the rival-exclusion guarantees would
  collapse, and a skeptic could say the discovered compound is an artifact of
  underdetermined supervision. You'd be trading a strong identifiability story
  for cosmetic resemblance to the experimental protocol.

  Cost, for completeness: it would be a substantial rework — your interpreter
  unfolds a per-frame transition from x[0], ECD's fn dispatch, the stitch
  rewriting, the MDL margin accounting, and all the rival-exclusion checks
  assume generative trajectory programs. New task type (trajectory-prefix →
  frame), new dispatch mode, re-run of both phases, and re-derivation of the
  margin numbers. Weeks, not days, and for the reasons above the payoff is
  negative.

  What I'd do instead (cheap): add a short "behavioral probe" section or figure.
  Take the discovered library, take a held-out Sally-Anne-style scene, show
  that the solved program's final frame answers the classic question — agent
  searches the believed location — while the shortest non-mental rival under the
  same library predicts the true location (naive answer). That's a page of
  exposition plus a trivial script reusing phase2_run.json solutions, and it
  makes the bridge to the developmental literature explicit: your learner,
  having compressed the corpus into a belief compound, passes the false-belief
  test; a learner restricted to the non-mental fragment fails it the way
  three-year-olds do. That's arguably a more compelling framing than
  reformatting the tasks, because it separates "what was learned" from "how it's
  tested" — exactly the distinction the developmental literature itself draws.
