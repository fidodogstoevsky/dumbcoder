⏺ Your curriculum maps onto the Wellman-Liu scale more cleanly than you might
  expect — and the one rung you're missing is diagnostic. Here's how I'd line it
  up, having looked at tasks_minds.py:

  The mapping. Your desire family (tasks_minds.py:168) is already a Diverse
  Desires analog: different (av, gv) combos mean different agents optimize
  toward different goals, and crucially these tasks are solvable with zero
  mental machinery — no fork, no private channel, just optimize parameterized
  differently per agent. That's exactly Wellman's claim about DD: it requires
  representing agents as having different connections to the world, not
  different representations of it. Knowledge Access maps onto your witness
  family and sync_to_world itself — "seeing leads to knowing" is literally what
  that combinator encodes, and the witness scenario (one agent saw the change,
  one didn't) is a KA vignette. False Belief is your center of mass:
  belief_wall, goal displacement, dual, false-obstacle.

  The missing rung is Diverse Beliefs, and it's missing for a structural reason 
  worth stating in the thesis. In Wellman's DB task, two people believe
  different things about an unknown reality — neither belief is marked true or
  false. In your setting the synthesizer always observes ground truth, so any
  belief divergence is immediately legible as a false belief; "merely different"
  beliefs aren't expressible without partial observability of the world itself.
  So your curriculum skips from DD straight to KA/FB. I wouldn't rush to patch
  this — it's a genuine and interesting difference between a psychometric scale
  (what a child can be tested on) and an MDL account (what an ideal observer
  with full scene access needs to compress). But it's the honest caveat to any
  claimed correspondence.

  On "learning to differentiate is taken for granted." Only half true, and the
  half that isn't is your strongest point of contact with the literature. What
  you take for granted is the capacity to differentiate — dup/mapsnd/fork
  machinery sits in the base language from the start. What you don't take for
  granted is its deployment: MDL only pays for a private channel when the corpus
  demands it, and (per your file17 result) never pays for more than one. That's
  structurally the same move as the competence/deployment debate in dev psych —
  infants show implicit false-belief sensitivity (Onishi & Baillargeon 2005)
  years before they pass elicited-response tasks, and processing-load accounts
  explain the gap as a cost of deploying a representation that already exists.
  Your framework is essentially a cost-based account where the cost is
  description length. Children on the Wellman scale might likewise be seen as
  progressively deciding the representational overhead of divergence is worth
  paying, rung by rung.

  How I'd actually compare them. The Wellman scale is a Guttman scale: an
  empirical difficulty ordering (pass DD before DB before KA before FB). Your
  framework can offer two quantitative analogs you already have infrastructure
  for:

  1. Discovery order — from the corpus_dl trajectory artifact, extract the round
  at which each family's compound enters the library / its tasks first solve,
  and check whether the emergent order is desire → witness → belief families.
  Note this must be the emergent solve order, not your designed corpus order —
  the scale describes what develops, not what's taught.
  2. Cardinal difficulty — your mdl_margin machinery gives nats-per-concept, so
  you can report the scale as a cost ladder rather than just an ordering: how
  many extra nats separates each rung's minimal program from the previous
  rung's.

  The inspiration worth taking. The scale ordering isn't culturally universal —
  in Chinese and Iranian samples, Knowledge Access precedes Diverse Beliefs
  (Wellman, Fang, Liu & colleagues, mid-2000s onward), which is awkward for any
  account that treats the order as logically necessary. Your framework predicts
  exactly this kind of variation: discovery order should depend on corpus
  statistics, not on conceptual necessity. Reweighting task-family frequencies
  and showing the discovery order reorder accordingly would be a cheap
  experiment with a strong payoff — it recasts the scale as
  environment-dependent compression order, which is a claim the developmental
  literature can't easily test but you can.

  If you only do one of these, I'd do the discovery-order figure first: it costs
  almost nothing (the trajectory artifacts already exist) and it either gives
  you a clean "the Wellman progression falls out of MDL" result or an
  interesting failure to explain.
