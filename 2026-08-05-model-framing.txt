⏺ This is a real question about the chapters' framing, and having read both
  chapters plus the upstream ones, my assessment is: you are at the model level 
  more than it feels like, and the "implementation-y" feel has identifiable 
  causes that are not the notation. Let me answer your three questions in order.

  Are we at the model level?

  Yes, in the sense that matters — and your own thesis already defines which
  sense that is. Chapter 2 states the computational-level claim and explicitly
  positions the system as an "algorithmic-level realisation" whose role is
  "sufficiency rather than fidelity." Chapter 1's constructivism section sets up
  the gap the thesis fills: the theory theory "does not say what process does
  the constructing," and the nativist's position is "parasitic on the absence of
  a mechanism." That means the contribution is the exhibited mechanism, and a
  mechanism is necessarily concrete. The concreteness of Chapter 4 isn't
  incidental detail obscuring a model — it's the load-bearing content, because
  the claim being answered is an existence claim ("no such mechanism exists").
  You can't refute that abstractly.

  The anxiety comes from conflating two senses of "model": cognitive model
  (which Chapter 4 is) and abstract description (which it partially isn't). A
  cognitive model of acquisition has to say exactly what the learner is given,
  shown, and optimizes — and those answers are unavoidably specific. The
  question is not "can we remove the specifics" but "does the reader always know
  which specifics carry the claim."

  Why it reads like implementation anyway

  Three causes, none of which is the mathematical notation:

  1. The model is never stated as one object. Chapter 4 presents its components
  in sequence — corpus, program space, primitives, search, compression — but
  never hands the reader the single sentence that makes them components of
  something: the model is a triple (initial language $L_0$, corpus $D$,
  description-length objective), the learner is the map from that triple to a
  final library, and the thesis's claim is that the belief-attribution schema is
  in the image of that map, reached through non-mental waypoints. Without that
  sentence up front, each section reads as an artifact being documented rather
  than a quantifier being instantiated. This is the cheapest fix with the
  biggest payoff.

  2. Inventory comes before principle. The primitives section reads as a parts
  catalog ("We have two ways of building a pair… four ways of transforming…"),
  and the corpus section as a data appendix (the 25-family tour). But in both
  cases the generative principle exists in the chapter — it just arrives late,
  as a defense. @granularity's "the kit is complete rather than curated — these
  maps are what having a product is" is a mathematical characterization of the
  library, not a design choice; stated first, it turns the list into a
  corollary. Same for the corpus: "each family should be readable as a claim on
  some corner of the language" is the model-level statement, and the tour drowns
  it. Derive, then list — or list in a table and spend the prose on the
  principle.

  3. There's no essential/incidental separation. A reader who hasn't read the
  code can't tell which details they're allowed to forget. Is the 5×5 grid
  essential? The ten cell values? The 3600-second budget? The product structure?
  Right now all are presented with equal weight. The model-level move is a
  short paragraph saying: essential are the product $G \times G$, the granted
  planner, the MDL objective, and the one-bag unlabeled corpus; incidental are
  grids, sizes, budgets, and every name the searcher prints. That one paragraph
  does more for a code-naïve reader than any amount of rewriting.

  What would talking at the model level actually look like?

  Here's the reframe that I think dissolves most of the worry: the programs are 
  not the implementation — they are the psychology. Your hypotheses are programs
  by the argument of Chapter 2 (language of thought, executability, countable
  parts). So $\pi_a^g$ is not code; it's the proposed content of the infant's
  teleological schema. $\mathrm{fork}(\pi_a^g \circ \mathrm{wall}_{r,c},
  \mathrm{sync}_a)$ is not code; it's the proposed structure of the mental state
  "Sally believes a wall is there," written in a candidate language of thought.
  When the prose carries that gloss, the reader experiences a theory of mental
  representation; when the gloss is missing, the same formula reads as
  implementation. Chapter 4 mostly has the glosses (the fork-anatomy figure, the
  agency-signature discussion, the no-BToM section are genuinely model-level
  writing). Where it lapses is exactly where it feels implementation-y: the
  composers compose_gp/pipe_gpg (pure plumbing, presented at the same altitude
  as the planner), the searcher's type names, the family tour.

  Concretely, model-level talk about this project would mean:

  - A worked Sally-Anne translation before any inventory. Open Chapter 4 with:
  here is the false-belief scene as the learner sees it (four scene renderings),
  here is the hypothesis it must eventually be able to write (the fork term,
  glossed clause by clause as "Sally believes…"), and here is the question (can
  a learner that starts without the word arrive at it, and in what order). You
  have this material — fig-fork-anatomy — but it sits 130 lines in, after the
  reader has already absorbed the corpus and library as unmotivated inventory.
  Everything after the worked example is then "filling in how," and the reader
  knows why each piece exists.
  - A dual-reader discipline: every section readable by a philosopher who skips
  every formula (the prose glosses alone must carry the argument) and by a
  technical reader who skips the prose (the formal statements must be complete).
  Most of Chapter 4 passes the first test; the primitives and composer
  paragraphs fail it. Demote what fails to a table or the appendix, or give it a
  one-line role-gloss ("each arrow needs its own composition operator; their
  names carry no content").
  - A grid-free statement of the model in two or three paragraphs before the
  instantiation: an observer over world-dynamics $W \to W$, with the product $W
  \times W$, a granted planner, and a two-part-code objective; then "we
  instantiate $W$ as small grids because scenes must be renderable and
  hypotheses enumerable." That's the intermediate level between Chapter 3's BToM
  and the concrete system, and it's what makes the essential/incidental line
  visible.

  On Chapter 5 specifically

  I'd largely acquit it. Results chapters are supposed to be concrete — the
  numbers are measurements of the model, and the arrow-notation convention you
  already adopted did the main lifting. The reading-guide table, the English
  names for fn_7, the "the arrow is what the abstraction is; the verbatim
  expression is what it cost" distinction — that's model-level reporting of a
  run. The residual implementation-feel there is run-bookkeeping (round-trip
  census caveats, machine symbol names), and that's honest reporting that should
  stay. If anything in Chapter 5 needs the treatment, it's making sure every
  quoted term gets its intentional-stance gloss on first use, which it mostly
  already does ("agent 1 believes 2 is one cell up; agent 6 knows better" is
  exactly the right move).

  The short version

  The problem isn't that the chapters are about the implementation — it's that
  Chapter 4 presents its components as found objects rather than as
  instantiations of a declared model, so the reader can't tell load-bearing from
  incidental. Three cheap additions fix most of it: a one-paragraph model
  statement (the triple and the claim about its image), a worked
  Sally-Anne-to-term translation moved to the front, and an explicit
  essential/incidental paragraph. Plus one reordering: state the "complete kit,
  nothing curated" and "each family claims a corner" principles before the lists
  they justify, not after as defenses.

  the primitives/corpus sections so you can judge them in place.
