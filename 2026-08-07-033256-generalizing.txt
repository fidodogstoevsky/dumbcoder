⏺ Your obstacle figure and the desire/belief contrast are the same mechanism at
  two different scales of $k$, and I think that's the sharpest way to say what
  you're circling.

  The formal fact underneath it. With a null perturbation, the belief
  construction and the desire solution are extensionally identical. Fork the
  world grid, don't touch the copy, run the planner against the copy, sync back
  the agent's position — you get exactly what optimize on the world gives you,
  frame for frame. So on desire scenes the mental and non-mental readings are
  not two hypotheses that make different predictions; they are two spellings of
  one function. Nothing in the data can separate them, ever. The only thing that
  separates them is description length: the belief spelling pays for the fork,
  the sync, and the identity, and buys nothing. That's why your learner never
  posits it, and it's the right result — a per-task MDL objective should refuse
  to pay for a slot it fills trivially.

  Why humans pay anyway. Your obstacle caption already contains the answer:
  "even though only scene 4 needs the wall, the program posits a wall in the
  other scenes as well." The wall is over-posited in scenes 1–3 because one
  program covers four scenes and it can't condition on which scene it's in. Now
  let $k$ be a lifetime rather than four. If a person runs one agent schema
  across every agent-episode they've ever observed, and some minority of those
  episodes are false-belief episodes that force a private-model slot, then the
  schema has the slot, and the slot gets filled in every episode — with the null
  perturbation in the overwhelming majority. Over-attribution of true belief
  isn't a separate psychological fact needing its own explanation. It's a slot
  forced by a minority of the corpus, filled degenerately across the rest,
  exactly as the wall is stamped into scenes 1–3.

  That framing also predicts something about the phenomenology, which I think is
  right: the true-belief attribution should feel contentless. Nobody watching
  someone cross a room to a coffee cup is actively representing what they
  believe. But ask, and the slot is there to be filled — because it's
  structurally present, not because it was doing work.

  There's a second, weaker mechanism worth distinguishing from the first: schema
  economy. Two productions (planner-on-world and planner-on-private-model) cost
  more to store than one that subsumes both. Uniformity is compressive at the
  library level even when it's wasteful per task. Your two-part objective
  already has the resources to say this — it's the same objective, just priced
  where the library lives rather than where the task does.

  The honest disanalogy, for limitations. Your endowment grants the planner as a
  primitive, so the non-mental route is permanently free and your learner
  cannot exhibit this over-generalization no matter how much of the corpus is
  false-belief. The mechanism is unavailable to it by construction. That's a
  real limit and a clean one to state: the model explains why belief attribution
  is discovered, not why it then spreads to cases that don't need it.

  If it'd help, I can measure the actual gap under the final library — price the
  null-perturbation belief spelling against the extensional solution on the
  desire family with the mdl_margin machinery. A number for "how many nats the
  uniform schema would cost you per non-mental task" would make the limitations
  paragraph concrete rather than hand-wavy.
