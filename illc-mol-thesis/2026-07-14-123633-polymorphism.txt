  Program Synthesis: The λ in the Machine                                       
  June 22, 2025                                                                 
  ai                                                                            
                                                                                
  Much of the "mainstream" discussion of artificial intelligence applied to     
  code generation is overwhelmingly dominated by the idea of scaling larger     
  langauge models and using test-time compute to improve model performance on   
  coding (SWE-bench, LiveCodeBench, etc) benchmarks. And that has legitimately  
  produced some very interesting results. Although I'm not sure how much more   
  juice there is to squeeze out of that fruit.                                  
                                                                                
  And indeed, the frontier models have demonstrated a remarkable capacity for   
  probabilistic mimicry and the ability to stich together and synthesize        
  templates of code from their training corpus in emergent and useful ways. But 
  they still operate by predicting token sequences, generating syntactically    
  plausible artifacts that often resemble human-written code. Whether they are  
  doing anything resembling reasoning is a highly debatable and open question   
  which I don't have any interesting opinions on because I truly don't know. I  
  do know that the current systems are limitedly useful in some cases, that     
  much seems undeniably true.                                                   
                                                                                
  Yet, I would still argue that to mistake this sophisticated pattern matching  
  for genuine program construction is a profound category error. Generating     
  code as a sequence of textual tokens using a statistical prior is a           
  fundamentally different task than generating programs by searching directly   
  in the space of program dynamics. The former is an act of linguistic surface  
  manipulation; the latter is an act of logical and semantic discovery. And     
  it's not clear to me that current models are capable of doing the latter.     
                                                                                
  But humour me for a moment and consider, that just perhaps the future of      
  AI-assisted development lies not in refining the models of today, but in      
  pursuing the more arduous, less-trodden path of program synthesis, a path     
  whose modern incarnation finds its deepest roots in the evolution of          
  functional programming and type theory. The ambition of program synthesis is  
  not to produce code that looks correct, but to construct a program that is,   
  by its very construction, proven to be correct against a specification. This  
  moves the locus of activity from the messy, ambiguous realm of natural        
  language prompts to the precise, verifiable domain of logical propositions.   
  This pursuit has historically been hampered by a computationally intractable  
  search space, but the maturation of functional programming, specifically with 
  the advent of dependent types, has provided much of the necessary             
  theoretical scaffolding.                                                      
                                                                                
  A dependently typed language allows specifications to be encoded directly     
  within the type system, transforming type-checking into a form of partial     
  program verification. This is where the concept of hole-style development     
  becomes the crucial user interface for this new paradigm. A programmer        
  architects the high-level structure, articulating properties as types, but    
  leaves specific implementation details as explicit "holes" in the code. These 
  are first-class, typed apertures in a well-formed program, and the            
  synthesizer's role is to find a term satisfying the rigid constraints of its  
  context.                                                                      
                                                                                
  Consider a dependently typed pseudocode for appending two vectors, where the  
  type system guarantees the length of the resulting vector. The programmer     
  might write the initial sketch, leaving the implementation logic as a hole,   
  denoted by ?.                                                                 
                                                                                
  data Vect : (Nat) -> (Type) -> Type where                                     
    Nil : Vect 0 a                                                              
    Cons : a -> Vect n a -> Vect (S n) a                                        
                                                                                
  append : (n m : Nat) -> Vect n a -> Vect m a -> Vect (n + m) a                
  append 0 m Nil ys = ys                                                        
  append (S n) m (Cons x xs) ys = Cons x ?                                      
                                                                                
  The type-checker deduces that this hole must be filled by a term of type Vect 
  (n + m) a. The synthesizer's task is now tremendously simplified. It does     
  not need to understand the "concept" of appending. It only needs to search    
  for a combination of operations that can transform the available terms (xs of 
  type Vect n a and ys of type Vect m a) into the required output type.         
  Through a guided search, it would discover that the recursive call append n m 
  xs ys satisfies the type, completing the program in a manner guaranteed to    
  be correct with respect to its type-level specification. If you've ever used  
  systems like Agda, Coq or Lean you've probably seen the power of this         
  approach.                                                                     
                                                                                
  This vision of collaborative, (albeit dynamically typed) synthesis is at      
  least partially realized in systems like DreamCoder represent a significant   
  step towards bridging symbolic reasoning with neural learning. DreamCoder     
  operates via a wake-sleep Bayesian learning cycle. In its "wake" phase, it    
  actively attempts to solve a corpus of inductive program synthesis problems,  
  searching for programs that satisfy given input-output examples. In its       
  "sleep" phase, it performs a kind of computational introspection: it analyzes 
  the solutions it found, refactoring and compressing common patterns into a    
  learned library of reusable, higher-level abstractions. Concurrently, it      
  trains a neural network to guide its future search, making the synthesis      
  process vastly more efficient. This iterative process of alternately solving  
  problems and then learning from those solutions allows the system to          
  bootstrap its own expertise, building an increasingly potent, domain-specific 
  language and a search policy tuned to its structure.                          
                                                                                
  The neural-guided approach pioneered by DreamCoder opens a still more         
  compelling research frontier: framing type-driven synthesis as a              
  reinforcement learning problem. One can imagine an agent, powered by an       
  algorithm like PPO, tasked with inductively constructing partial programs and 
  filling holes in a program. The environment is the incomplete program; the    
  state is the typed hole and its local context; the available actions are the  
  primitives in the current library, which itself may have been learned. A      
  reward is given for any action that produces a type-correct term, with a      
  significant final reward for completing the program. The agent's policy       
  network would learn a semantic prior, predicting not which token is likely to 
  come next, but which branch or insert is most likely to satisfy a given       
  local type signature and the global goal of optimizing towards a reward       
  function that maximizes coherence to the specification. This moves beyond     
  pattern matching on surface syntax and toward learning the logical structure  
  of a problem domain, a direction explored by research into systems like       
  DeepSynth, LambdaBeam and other neural-guided deductive search methods.       
                                                                                
  This kind of fun speculation is fun, and it's an very interesting and open    
  research problem. But a sober dose of realism is required. Despite the        
  intellectual elegance of the idea of program synthesis, its future is far     
  from assured. The development of these synthesizers would be a monumental     
  engineering task with very little short-term economic upside realized by its  
  developers, probably making it unfundable in any traditional model. Each      
  domain, each library, each API must be furnished with formal specifications   
  or a well-curated set of problems for the synthesizer to be effective. This   
  is a stark contrast to statistical models, which can ingest the chaotic,      
  undocumented, and inconsistent sprawl of existing ecosystems like NPM or PyPI 
  and still extract useful, albeit brittle, patterns of usage.                  
                                                                                
  The network effects of existing programming ecosystems are a force of nature  
  that technical purity has consistently failed to overcome. This was the       
  bitter lesson learned by the Lisp and functional programming communities over 
  the last forty years. While they built pristine, powerful, and                
  self-consistent bubble worlds, they were ultimately outcompeted by the        
  pragmatic secretion and accretion of C, Java, and Python, whose value derived 
  not from internal coherence but from the sheer scale of their surrounding     
  libraries and economic incentives. Thus I would probalby condede that for the 
  foreseeable future, the industry will be dominated by the superficially       
  effective approach of statistical token prediction. The dream of provably     
  correct programs, synthesized through a formal dialogue between human and     
  machine, will continue to be a inspiring vision, but it may be dwarfed by the 
  immense gravitational pull of code reuse and the path of least resistance     
  offered by simply mimicking what has been written before. But we can still    
  dream of DreamCoder future.

  program must prove. There's no Nat-indexed family, no type that varies with a
  value, no type-checking-as-verification. Your own comment even calls it "the
  monomorphic type system has no generic [composer]" — the opposite of
  dependent.
  - No correctness guarantee. The article's whole point is programs "proven
  correct by construction." Your programs are empirically correct (they match
  the corpus); a stitch-invented abstraction is an MDL win, not a theorem.

  So if you ask "am I doing dependently-typed, hole-driven,
  correct-by-construction synthesis?" — no. You're doing neurally-un-guided,
  type-pruned, example-driven, compression-based synthesis. The article would
  file you under "DreamCoder," and it explicitly frames DreamCoder as the
  dynamically typed realization, conceding the type-theoretic dream is
  elsewhere.

  The deeper reason the comparison misfires

  Even where the machinery rhymes, the telos is orthogonal. The article is about
  correctness — synthesis as a route to provably-correct software, an
  engineering end in itself. Your project doesn't care about correctness at all;

  article never imagines that use — its entire frame is "build correct programs

⏺ Now I've got the full picture of your type machinery. Let me answer
  concretely.

  What your type system actually is

  Bare-string, monomorphic, first-order, exact-match. A type is either a ground
  string (grid, int, dir, coord) or an opaque arrow name (fn, sfn, pair_gg,
  fn_p_g, fn_g_p, fn_p_p, fn_g_c, fn_gc_g). Delta.arrow = (tuple(tailtypes), 
  type) (dsl.py:716), and enumeration resolves a hole by possibles = 
  D.bytype[tailtype] — a dict keyed on string identity (ecd.py:132). Invented
  abstractions get types by reading concrete hole types straight off the body
  via typize (ecd.py:506). There is no type variable and no unification
  anywhere.

  What polymorphism buys in DreamCoder — and which parts apply to you

  DreamCoder's polymorphism does three things. Two don't matter for you; one
  matters a lot.

  1. Search-space pruning via unification — doesn't matter here. Polymorphic
  request types let unification kill ill-typed branches early. But your DSL is
  ~30 primitives and your bottleneck isn't branching factor; monomorphic dict
  lookup is already maximally tight. You'd gain ~nothing on speed.

  2. One generic primitive instead of N monomorphic copies — matters, but 
  narrowly. This is the real one, and it lands exactly on your sore spot: the
  product-category combinator family. Look at what you were forced to write:

  dup_g       : grid -> pair_gg
  mapsnd      : fn   -> fn_p_p
  compose_gp  : fn_g_p, fn_p_p -> fn_g_p
  pipe_gpg    : fn_g_p, fn_p_g -> fn

  These are monomorphic shadows of universal categorical combinators:

  dup     : a -> pair a a            (the diagonal Δ)
  mapsnd  : (b -> c) -> pair a b -> pair a c   (bifunctor 'second')
  compose : (b -> c) -> (a -> b) -> (a -> c)

  Your own comment at dsl.py:324 says it outright — "the monomorphic type system
  has no generic composer." You wrote compose_gp and pipe_gpg and the
  fn_g_p/fn_p_p family precisely because you couldn't say compose once.
  Polymorphism collapses that whole family into the three standard combinators.

  3. Cross-instantiation abstraction reuse — matters most, and it's 
  thesis-relevant. With monomorphic types, a stitch abstraction discovered at
  type grid can only be reused at grid. With polymorphism, an abstraction
  abstracted over ∀a reuses across instantiations by construction. That is
  structurally exactly what file16 is trying to demonstrate empirically (one
  joint stitch over minds-free and minds tasks; belief as a shared MDL win, not
  a silo artefact) and what file17's cons/nil stack hand-engineers (arity as a
  free parameter). Polymorphism would make both of those fall out of the type 
  discipline instead of being argued task-by-task or built by hand.

  The catch that's specific to your thesis

  Here's the part you actually need to weigh, because it cuts against the
  obvious "yes, cleaner types are better":

  Polymorphism changes your MDL baseline. Collapsing the monomorphic
  product-category family into three generic combinators shrinks the library's 
  description length. If belief currently wins the MDL comparison partly because
  the channel-plumbing combinators are individually "expensive" in the prior, a
  smaller polymorphic library moves the goalposts. You'd have to re-run
  file16/file17's comparisons. The good news: it moves them in the honest
  direction — a smaller, more standard, more universal primitive set is a
  stronger baseline to beat, so if belief still wins, the result is more
  convincing, not less. But it's a re-run, not a free lunch, and file17 already
  reached a verdict.

  There's also the argumentative upside that I think is the real prize: right
  now a skeptic can say "your dup/mapsnd/commit combinators are bespoke 
  belief-plumbing — of course belief decomposes into them." If those combinators
  are literally the generic categorical primitives compose/dup/pair that any
  DSL would carry, that objection evaporates. Belief becomes a compound of
  genuinely universal operations. That's synthesis-not-primitive made airtight.

  My recommendation

  Two tiers, and I'd lead with the cheap one because you're past file17:

  Cheap (do this regardless): defend the decomposition as polymorphic in 
  principle without reimplementing. In the thesis, exhibit the unifying
  polymorphic types for the product-category family and state that your
  monomorphic primitives are its ground instances at a = grid. You get ~80% of
  the parsimony argument — "belief decomposes into standard categorical
  combinators, not bespoke primitives" — at zero engineering cost and zero MDL
  re-run. This neutralizes the bespoke-plumbing objection directly.

  Full (only if you want file16/17's reuse claims to be structural, not 
  empirical): implement real polymorphism. Worth it only if the cross-corpus
  generalization story is central enough that you want it to hold by 
  construction rather than by re-running stitch. It's a bounded but non-trivial
  rewrite, and it invalidates your current numbers.

  If you do the full version — implementation sketch

  Your architecture is actually friendly to this because stitch stays syntactic
  — it compresses normalized programs and you re-infer types afterward
  (ecd.py:302–310, typize). So stitch never needs to know about polymorphism.
  The surgery is four localized pieces:

  1. Type terms. Replace bare strings with a tiny ADT: ground ('con', 'grid', 
  []), variable ('var', 'a'), applied ('con', 'pair', [t1, t2]). Keep arrows as
  (args, ret). Ground types stay strings if you want minimal churn — just add a
  TVar sentinel.
  2. Robinson unification. Add unify(t1, t2, subst), apply(subst, t), and
  instantiate(t) (rename a primitive's bound vars fresh per occurrence). ~40
  lines.
  3. Enumeration — the one real change (makepaths/cenumerate/penumerate,
  ecd.py:120–225). Replace possibles = D.bytype[tailtype] (exact dict) with: for
  request type τ, candidates are primitives whose instantiated return type
  unifies with τ; thread the resulting substitution into that primitive's
  tailtypes before recursing into its holes. Keep a coarse index keyed on the
  outermost type constructor for a fast pre-filter, then unify to confirm — with
  ~30 primitives a linear unify scan per hole is completely fine. The Q/logp
  weighting is untouched (still per-primitive).
  4. Abstraction inference (typize/_unsatisfied_tailtypes, ecd.py:485–518). This
  is what delivers the reuse. Instead of reading concrete hole types off the
  body, run HM over the body: fresh var per hole, unify through the spine, then
  generalize the free vars in the result → a polymorphic arrow for the invented
  fn_i. The partial-application hole-injection logic (ecd.py:486–504) needs to
  unify rather than string-compare, but the structure is the same.
  5. Re-ground the primitives. Rewrite the product-category family
  (dsl.py:286–353) as dup : a->pair a a, mapsnd : (b->c)->pair a b->pair a c,
  compose/pipe as the generic categorical composers, plus pair/fst/snd. Delete
  compose_gp, pipe_gpg, fn_g_p, fn_p_p as separate primitives — they become
  instances. This is where the library physically shrinks.

  The honest summary: polymorphism buys you almost nothing on search and a real
  amount on argument — and the argument it strengthens (universal combinators +
  by-construction cross-task reuse) is precisely the one your thesis is making.
  Given your stage, I'd take the in-principle framing now and reserve the full
  rewrite for a "future work / it generalizes" section unless a committee member
  pushes on the bespoke-combinator objection.

❯ is there any argumentative advantage to having fork and sync decomposed, over 
  just having them atomic?                                                      

⏺ Yes — and it's not a refinement, it's the move that makes the whole
  anti-nativist claim actually bite rather than quietly beg the question. Let me
  lay out why, and then the honest cost, because the advantage comes with a
  debt you're already paying in file14/file16.

  The regress that atomic fork can't escape

  Phase 1 (atomic fork/sync) establishes: Believes is not a primitive — it's a
  learned composition of fork/sync plus policies. Good. But a nativist doesn't
  even break stride:

  ▎ Fine. Believes isn't your primitive. But fork is. fork(derive, commit) w = 
  ▎ commit(w, derive(w)) opens a private channel for an agent, runs a decoupled 
  ▎ model in it, and reconciles it — that is exactly Leslie's M-representation: 
  ▎ the decoupling mechanism of the ToMM, the proprietary metarepresentational 
  ▎ endowment. You renamed my innate module fork and declared it learned. You 
  ▎ relocated the primitive one level down; you didn't eliminate it.

  This retreat is decisive against atomic fork, because the entire
  metarepresentational signature — agency, decoupling, the informational
  relation — sits inside one opaque token. The explanandum is packed into a
  primitive. Phase 1 alone is question-begging in precisely the way Scholl &
  Leslie would press.

  What decomposition does to that regress

  When fork becomes commit ∘ mapsnd(derive) ∘ dup, the metarepresentational
  content is no longer in any primitive. Look at what the parts are:

  - dup : a → a × a — the diagonal. "Make a copy."
  - mapsnd : (b→c) → a×b → a×c — bifunctor second. "Transform only the copy."
  - commit — the product eliminator. "Reconcile."

  Leslie's decoupling — the supposedly sui-generis act of holding a
  representation apart from reality so it can be operated on without
  truth-evaluation — reduces to dup then mapsnd. Copy the world, transform the
  copy, leave the original. That is the most domain-general operation
  imaginable; it is what you do to render a hover-preview or an undo buffer. The
  mystery of metarepresentation dissolves into copying-and-mapping. The agency
  signature is now an emergent property of a particular composition of
  type-agnostic combinators, with no primitive home.

  That is the argumentative payload: decomposition is what converts your system 
  from "belief is a compound of mental primitives" into "belief is a compound of
  non-mental primitives." Only the second defeats nativism, because only the
  second denies the nativist a primitive to point at. This is the constructivist
  thesis stated at full strength — the residual innate endowment is
  general-purpose compositional structure (a cartesian category), not a
  concept-specific module. The constructivist never denied innate machinery; she
  denied innate proprietary machinery. Decomposition is exactly the maneuver
  that pushes the irreducible commitment down to the banal, conceded level
  (product/composition structure) and out of the contested one (a ToM module).

  The existence-proof framing (this is the real spine)

  Underneath, your thesis is a poverty-of-the-stimulus rebuttal: nativism argues
  belief-attribution can't be assembled from domain-general resources, ∴
  innate. You answer with an existence proof — here is a domain-general route.
  But the force of an existence proof is exactly the generality of the resources
  it consumes. An existence proof that helps itself to fork proves nothing the
  nativist cares about, because fork is the thing whose constructibility is in
  dispute. An existence proof that bottoms out in dup/mapsnd/compose —
  combinators you independently use in the minds-free tasks (file14's
  overlay/registration, file16's mixed corpus) — actually rebuts the
  poverty-of-stimulus argument, because the resources are demonstrably not
  belief-specific. Decomposition is what makes your existence proof valid rather
  than circular.

  The cost decomposition incurs — be honest about it

  Decomposition is not free; it takes on two debts that atomic fork doesn't:

  1. The independent-motivation debt. The instant the agency signature lives in
  a composition rather than a primitive, you're exposed to the smuggling charge:
  "you reverse-engineered the combinators to hit the belief target." The only
  discharge is showing the combinators are fixed by work that has nothing to do
  with minds — which is exactly why file14 gives fork/sync non-mental tasks and
  file16 runs one joint stitch over the mixed corpus. Those aren't side-quests;
  they're the premise that licenses the decomposition. Atomic fork never owes
  this.
  2. The learnability stakes go up. Decomposition makes the belief solution
  deeper in program space — which the PLAN wants ("false-belief solutions live
  deeper"), because depth is the formal correlate of developmental lateness. But
  depth is only a virtue if compression actually re-discovers the belief-shaped
  composition as an MDL win over the mixed corpus. If stitch doesn't recover
  it, you haven't shown belief is a learnable abstraction — you've shown it's an
  unlearnably deep accident. Atomic fork is safer (belief is shallow, trivially
  reachable) but proves correspondingly less. Decomposition bets the thesis on
  file16's compression result. That's the right bet, but name it as a bet.

  The soft spot: sync stays atomic

  This is the part I'd nail down before a committee does. file15 decomposes fork
  but keeps sync atomic "to preserve the shared-av agency signature," even
  though sync_decomposed = register(locate(v), place(v)) exists in the DSL. So a
  sharp reader asks: if you decompose fork to dodge the relocation regress, 
  doesn't keeping sync atomic reopen the regress at sync? Is sync_to_world now
  the relocated nativist primitive — the residue where all the irreducibly
  agentive content concentrated?

  You have a defensible answer, but you have to make it, and you have to pick
  which one:

  - Legibility, not irreducibility: sync can be decomposed (you have the
  identity), you keep it atomic only so the shared-av binding stays readable;
  the anti-nativist claim rests on decomposability-in-principle, which you've
  shown. This is honest and matches the polymorphic-in-principle posture you
  took for the combinators. I'd take this one.
  - Irreducibility: the reconciliation-on-a-shared-agent-value is the genuine
  minimal core of agency and should be primitive. This is a stronger, more
  interesting claim — but it's a partial concession to nativism (there is one
  small proprietary primitive after all), and you'd need to argue av-indexed
  reconciliation is still general-purpose (it's just "write the prediction
  back," used in registration too).

  Either is fine; an unaddressed asymmetry is not.

  Bottom line

  The advantage is real and central: atomic fork/sync proves only that Believes
  is non-primitive given a mental primitive, which a nativist absorbs by
  relocation. Decomposition proves belief is assembled from non-mental, 
  independently-motivated, domain-general parts — the one result that actually
  closes the regress and turns your existence proof into a valid
  poverty-of-stimulus rebuttal. The price is that it only pays off if (a) the
  parts are fixed by minds-free tasks and (b) compression re-discovers the
  belief composition as an MDL win — which is precisely what file14 and file16
  are for. So decomposition isn't a stylistic upgrade over atomic; it's the
  difference between relocating the nativist's primitive and eliminating it.

❯ So is the atomic fork even useful at all? Isn't any point made by atomic fork 
  also made by the decomposition?                                               
⏺ Your instinct is mostly right, and it's worth saying so plainly: as a 
  standalone positive argument, atomic fork is subsumed. Every positive claim it
  makes — Believes is a compound, belief is a relation over an informational
  state, false-belief solutions are assembled not innate — the decomposition
  makes too, and makes better, because it makes them without the smuggled
  primitive. If atomic fork were only there to prove "belief is composed," you
  should cut it.

  But it isn't only there for that. There are three residual roles where atomic
  fork does work decomposition cannot do on its own, and one of them is
  load-bearing.

  1. It's the control for a comparative claim

  The PLAN's claim "false-belief solutions live deeper in program space" is
  comparative — deeper than what? Depth-in-program-space is only meaningful
  against a baseline. Atomic fork is that baseline: it's the condition where
  belief is shallow (fork + a policy). The decomposition is the condition where
  it's deep. You need both conditions to instantiate the measurement; a single
  decomposed run gives you a depth number with nothing to compare it to. So
  atomic fork earns its keep as the ablation arm, even though it makes no
  positive argument itself. This is real and you can't get it from decomposition
  alone — by construction.

  2. It's the foil that makes the demystification legible

  The punchline of the decomposition — "Leslie's decoupling is just dup then
  mapsnd, copy-and-map" — only lands as a demystification if the reader first
  saw decoupling looking primitive. The atomic reading is where decoupling looks
  like a sui-generis metarepresentational act; the decomposed reading is where
  it dissolves. Drop the atomic version and "decoupling reduces to copy-and-map"
  has nothing to reduce from — you've answered an objection the reader was
  never set up to feel. This is expository, not strictly argumentative, but a
  poverty-of-stimulus rebuttal is rhetorically a before/after: here is the thing
  that looks unlearnable, here is it being learned. Atomic fork is the
  "before."

  3. The one that matters: atomic fork should be an output, not an axiom

  Here's where I'd push back on the dichotomy itself. You're framing it as
  "atomic OR decomposed — pick one." For a DreamCoder system that's a false
  choice, and resolving it rescues exactly what's valuable about atomic fork.

  The right experiment is: primitives are the decomposed combinators; fork is 
  something compression re-invents. Start the library with
  dup/mapsnd/compose/commit, run the joint stitch over the mixed corpus (this is
  just file16), and show that the system re-discovers a fork-shaped abstraction
  — because it's reused across many tasks — and then invents Believes on top of
  the invented fork. That single result gives you everything:

  - fork is non-primitive (it's invented, not stipulated) → anti-nativism ✓
  - fork is nonetheless a real, stable, reused rung (it earns its MDL keep) →
  the two-channel structure is genuine, not an artefact ✓
  - Believes sits on the discovered fork, which sits on domain-general parts →
  the hierarchy is the evidence of modular structure ✓

  So the content of "atomic fork" — that there's a meaningful intermediate
  two-channel rung belief depends on — survives and is in fact proven better
  this way than by stipulating it. What dies is only atomic fork as a starting 
  axiom. And notice: this is exactly the trajectory your own project already
  took. file13 stipulates fork; file16/17 push toward discovering structure (the
  joint stitch, "MDL never selects >1 channel"). Your instinct that atomic fork
  is redundant is the same instinct that drove 13 → 17.

  The tradeoff you'd lose by cutting it entirely

  There's also a subtler reason not to throw atomic fork away, which cuts
  against "decomposition strictly dominates":

  The decomposed result risks proving too much. If your primitives are a
  near-universal combinatory basis, then "domain-general combinators can express
  belief" is almost trivial — everything is expressible in a complete basis.
  Mere expressibility is not the thesis; discoverability under MDL is. Atomic
  fork, by contrast, lives in a constrained space — fork/sync are a structured
  two-channel capacity, not a universal basis — so "given this capacity, belief
  is the short/natural composition" is a more informative positive result. So
  there's a genuine tradeoff, not dominance:

  - Atomic: stronger "belief is natural/short," weaker anti-nativism (constraint
  looks smuggled).
  - Decomposed: stronger anti-nativism, weaker "belief is natural"
  (expressibility is cheap in a rich basis — rescued only by the MDL/compression
  result).

  The rediscovery experiment in §3 is what collects both upsides: no smuggled
  primitive and belief-as-compression-win rather than mere expressibility, with
  the fork-rung validated by independent reuse.

  Bottom line

  Cut atomic fork as a co-equal positive existence proof — there you're right,
  it's subsumed and including it as such invites the "you just stipulated the
  capacity" charge against your own setup. Keep it in exactly three roles: (1)
  the baseline for the program-depth comparison, (2) the foil that makes
  "decoupling = copy-and-map" demystify something, and (3) reframe it as a
  rediscovery target — the abstraction compression should re-invent from the
  decomposed primitives, not assume. In the final argument "atomic fork" appears
  as a waypoint the system discovers, not a floor you lay down. That honors
  your instinct without losing the one thing atomic fork was actually tracking:
  that the two-channel rung is real.
